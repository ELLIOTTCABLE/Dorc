### § 1.2-fnd-sole-route-seated-on-the-blind-child (wrong DISJOINT or no DISJOINT; brief's questions 2 and 3)

The book is the most ordinary one there is, ten config files, one drifted:

```sh
#!/bin/sh
set -eu
install -m 644 ./nginx.conf   /etc/nginx/nginx.conf         # 3   drifted today: really runs
install -m 644 ./site.conf    /etc/nginx/conf.d/site.conf   # 4   converged
install -m 600 ./dhparam.pem  /etc/nginx/dhparam.pem        # 5   converged
install -m 644 ./sshd_config  /etc/ssh/sshd_config          # 6   converged
```

Under `--risk-faultless-skips`, lines 4–6 survive line 3 only if the chokepoint says DISJOINT for two files of one sort. §3.2's DISJOINT bullet: legs below the deepest SAME level (the filesystem), "DISJOINT iff every mKey of both legs carries :sole-route for its shape and ... the two legs' tops are mKeys of one mScheme, each carrying :guarantees-unique-name, with differing values". The legs here are single inodes, so the inode leaf must carry `:sole-route`.

Who says it, per the text. §2.2: "P's owner declares, per matched shape of the mKey's value, :identified-in mSort M ... and ... :sole-route — mReferents reached by mKeys of this shape are reachable only through their mParent", and §1.6: "declared by the primary mScheme's owner per matched shape of the mKey's value, a function of the mKey's own bytes". P is `sm.Inode`; its owner is Tessa; the shape is matched against an inode number. An inode number's bytes carry no information about whether its filesystem is ext4 on a disk, the lower layer of an overlay, an NFS export also served to a second mount on this host, or sshfs synthesising numbers per client. §2.2 says so itself two paragraphs later: "the child mSort's owner never learns the mParent's types". So Tessa has one shape, `*`, and one choice:

- declare `:sole-route` on every inode. §2.2 licenses it "by constitution (... an inode in its ext4 table)". It is false wherever the filesystem is a lower layer of an overlay (every docker host: `/var/lib/docker/overlay2/*/diff` is ext4, and its inodes are reachable through the merged view), and false for any directory exported over NFS and mounted back on the same host. The model's own leg rule then needs the *other* leg to lack sole-route to stay UNKNOWN, which it does only if the filesystem-level classifier recognised "overlay" — from a device number, which it cannot (§1.6 again: a function of the bytes; major 0 is nfs, tmpfs, overlay, btrfs subvolumes and procfs alike). `312b` §8 punted overlayfs to its own exercise with exactly this worry ("else a write to the lower spares wrongly through the merged path").
- declare nothing. Then no two files are ever DISJOINT, lines 4–6 guard on every drifted morning, and stage 5's payoff is gone for the most common line in every book.

`312b` §7 already saw this against 311: "`:sole-route` is the mSort-PrimaryStore's knowledge (store or view); the parent declares once, children inherit; 311 puts it on the child." 311j §2.2 still puts it on the child and adds the sentence "mParent classification and :sole-route are one declaration", which reads as if the fix were taken but is about the child classifying its parent's mSort, not the parent declaring the child's route. The rewrite carried the wrong seat forward and papered the seam.

The same rule read against processes. §2.2 lists "a process in its kernel" as sole-route by constitution; §4.2 says nested pid namespaces killed deriving sole-route from chain shape because "guest pid 1 and host pid 4821 are one mReferent". Take a procps author at their word:

```sh
kill -HUP "$(cat /run/nginx.pid)"        # 3   pid 4821 in the root namespace
docker exec web kill -HUP 1              # 6   the same process, seen as pid 1 inside
```

If the pid mScheme is identified in the pid namespace (the only parent under which `:guarantees-unique-name` is true), then `:sole-route` is false for every pid shape, because every process is reachable through each ancestor namespace. With no sole-route on the leaf, two pids in one namespace are never DISJOINT, which is safe and useless. If the author instead follows §2.2's example and identifies pids in the boot with sole-route and unique-name, line 3's process and line 6's read DISJOINT (differing values, one scheme, sole-route, unique-name), and a converged fact about one survives a signal to the other: wrong DISJOINT, and it is the §4.2 case verbatim. The rule can be satisfied only by a lie or by giving up.

The direction of the diff. 311 §3.2 spent sole-route on the levels *above* the divergence. On this book that gives line 3 versus line 6 DISJOINT at the namespace level (namespaces differ, unique-name, nothing above) — wrong, since the two are one process. 311j spends it on every leg key including the tops. On two pids in one namespace that gives UNKNOWN — right but only by refusing the case. Neither is the rule the world needs, which is: unique-name at the tops separates siblings; sole-route is needed on leg keys *strictly below* the tops, so that nothing reached through a deeper key is also reachable through the other top. That rule gives DISJOINT for two pids in one namespace (tops are the pids; no keys below) and UNKNOWN for a pid against a pid in a child namespace (the leg below the namespace top is a pid without sole-route). +SURE of both walks; ~SUSPECT the corrected rule has no further hole (it was not hunted).

### § 1.3-fnd-cell-fold-lost-the-reboot-derivation (wrong SAME across a lifecycle boundary; brief's question 2)

§1.9 says two things about one example that cannot both be true: "a mCell's identity is its mSort-Bearer's plus which property" and "Two mCells of one mSort-Bearer (`enabled`, in a symlink's existence; `active`, in pid 1's memory) ... their mFullyQualifiedKeys differ exactly where one survives a reboot and the other does not, with no declaration about reboots by anyone." One bearer has one chain; two cells whose identity is that chain plus a property differ nowhere above the property. Whether the chain passes through the boot is decided once, by the bearer's owner, for both cells.

311 could derive the sentence: §1.8 and §2.7 gave each mAspectSort "its own `resolve()`, mKey-PrimaryStore, mPlacements, and :observer-dependence", so `enabled` was identified in the filesystem holding the wants-symlink and `active` in pid 1's table in the boot, and §3.3's lifecycle rule did the rest. The fold to "a mCell is an mSort with a singleton key under its bearer" dropped the per-cell primary store and kept the sentence. `312b` §9's own sketch ("`sm.UnitEnablement` under `sm.UnitFile:nginx`") quietly puts the cells under two *different* bearers, a unit file and a unit, which is the 311 shape again under another name and is not what §1.9 says.

The book is the patch day the model itself cites (`30W` §7):

```sh
#!/bin/sh
set -eu
apt-get -y dist-upgrade                       # 3
systemctl start postgresql-exporter           # 4   converged: active (a unit deliberately not enabled)
systemctl enable nginx                        # 5   converged: enabled
reboot                                        # 6   the lifecycle line
systemctl start postgresql-exporter           # 8   after the reboot: must run; the unit is down
```

The systemd author must pick the bearer's parent. Pick the unit table in the boot (the safe pick): line 8's fact perishes at line 6 (§3.3, lifecycle), line 8 runs; line 5's `enabled` perishes too and is re-probed for nothing; §1.9's "one survives a reboot" is false but harmless. Pick the unit file in the filesystem (the pick §1.9's own example invites, since "enabled, in a symlink's existence" is the fact that survives): line 8's chain does not pass through the boot, its `active` fact measured at line 4's standup stands, line 8 is elided, the exporter stays down. Wrong SAME across a boundary the model claims to handle "with no declaration about reboots by anyone". +SURE of the walk; ~SUSPECT how the engine sequences a book across a reboot at all, which is `30W`'s business, not this model's; the finding is the text's false derivation and the choice it hides.

### § 1.4-fnd-silence-is-the-widest-claim (wrong SAME across a transition; brief's question 3)

Two places in the model where the line that says least claims most, against the naming law §2 itself invokes ("dangerous operations rare and long-named; safe ones short").

The parentless warrant. §1.6: "A shape with no `:identified-in` is scoped in the mRoute"; §1.5: a warrant holds "for every mKey that takes it inside any one mParent"; §3.4 and the exercise (`obs-the-sentinel-is-the-keystone`): the route and the boot are inherited through a wrapper's `lends nothing-else`. Put together: a warrant declared on a shape whose owner named no parent is a claim scoped in the route, and the route is one instance on both sides of every wrapper anyone will ever write. The narrowest-looking declaration is the widest one in the system. The exercise uses this deliberately for `sm.BootId` and says the stdlib owns the horizon. A stranger uses it by accident:

```sh
#!/bin/sh
set -eu
sudo -u deploy crontab /opt/deploy/deploy.cron        # 3   deploy's crontab, from a file
crontab /opt/deploy/root.cron                          # 4   root's, from another file
```

A crontab oracle at the floor binds nothing from argv that names the crontab; the referent is selected by the invoking user and the key is a singleton (`: asserts "sm.CrontabFloor:current"`, no parent). At the floor, line 3 against line 4 is two placeholders (different entry chains) with equal values and no warrant: UNKNOWN, correct. Now the author, who knows perfectly well that `crontab -l` for one user always answers with that user's one crontab, writes the true-sounding line `warrants guarantees-unique-referent`. The route is inherited through sudo's sentinel; the leaf values are equal; the shape carries the warrant; §3.2 bullet 3 says SAME. Whichever of lines 3 and 4 the standup measured converged stands in for the other, and one of the two crontabs is never installed. The sentence "one key, one crontab, per user" was true; the model read it as "per route". The person who knew the tool said something true and the model made it a claim about sudo, containers, and every wrapper written after them.

The remedy is one rule the model does not have and the exercise does not need: a warrant is declarable only on a shape that has an `:identified-in`; a route-scoped shape's equal keys are SAME only as one placeholder within a transit-free span (§1.10's engine vouch), never by warrant. This costs nothing in the exercise: lines 7 and 8 read SAME through the inherited instance ("one placeholder and no warrant is consulted", `obs-identical-instances-need-no-warrant`), and the cross-vantage comparison the BootId warrant would have licensed is already UNKNOWN by §1.10 ("across mVantages it is unknown"). +SURE of the walk; ~SUSPECT that authors will write warrants on floor shapes often (the model's own culture pushes against it; the point is that the model does not refuse it, and the sentence is true where the author stands).

The sentinel. `lends nothing-else` is, in the exercise's words, "the cheapest line in the set" and "carries the most". What it asserts is that this wrapper leaves untouched the instance of every mParent-Catalog mSort anyone has declared or will declare, except the ones named. A wrapper author knows what their tool changes; they cannot know which of those changes some leaf author elsewhere has made a catalog (§3.4: "A lend may depend on the guest"; GOTCHA 34, sudo scrubbing what an outer `env` lent; GOTCHA 36, `-l` sourcing profiles). The model's defence is that a floor leaf through a wrapper is UNKNOWN regardless (placeholders keyed by entry chain), which held under every attack tried here (§2 below). The residue is the same as the paragraph above: a leaf that carries a warrant and did not name the catalog the wrapper actually moved. The two findings are one: silence about a parent is read as "the route", and the route is the strongest possible scope. The naming law wants it to be the weakest.

## § 2-attacks-that-held
