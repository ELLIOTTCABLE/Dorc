# turn 6 — 2026-08-23 19:06:00 CDT

Front: `front-commitment-vs-digest`. Public hashes versus keyed commitments for document
identity and opaque-field commitments, especially over low-entropy values. Mid-turn the
conductor added a naming steer: the human leans toward human-readable store filenames with
a project-minted normalisation discipline, which sharpens the two naming questions.

## Findings

- **A keyless document cannot commit to a low-entropy value. This is a clean negative
  result and it is over-determined** — three independent sources reach the same endpoint by
  different routes. The keyless option space collapses to exactly two points: publish the
  salt beside the digest (guess-confirmable in one hash per guess), or discard the salt (the
  output is a random tag with no relationship to the value, so nobody — including the owner —
  can ever open it). There is no third keyless point. The reason a *keyless salted commitment*
  works in the one standard that uses it is that the salt is withheld until disclosure and
  lives outside the published artefact; a projection whose entire premise is that it carries
  no protected material has nowhere to withhold it to.
- **Therefore: the plain projection must not emit any value-derived output for an
  opaque-value-capable field. Not a digest, not a truncated digest, not a salted digest.**
  What it may emit is a per-document random tag per distinct value, minted from the injected
  randomness seam and bearing no cryptographic relationship to the value. That buys exactly
  one thing — within-document equality classes ("these four sites saw the same value") — at
  zero confidentiality cost, and it is the same thing the standard advice arrives at
  independently ("why not just assign random IDs … use the salt itself as the ID. No need to
  use a hash function").
- **The tag's scope is the document and only the document, and this is a hard boundary, not
  a preference.** A shipped per-scope-salt scheme was broken exactly here: the scope salt was
  an in-band file the adversary could copy into a scope they controlled, after which
  determinism inside the scope became a matching oracle. Read across: anyone holding the plain
  document can already see which tags repeat; they learn nothing about *what* repeated. That
  is the whole and only guarantee. Do not describe it as more.
- **"Shape metadata is safe" is false, and it is false with numbers.** Chunk counts and
  lengths alone — no value bytes at all — leak at a measured ~1/30,000 to ~1/100,000 of the
  data; over a 1.6 MB object that is ≥128 bits, "universally uniquely identifiable in the
  sense of UUIDs". Measured instances: the first two compressed chunk lengths uniquely
  identified the song across a 10,000-song library; ~20% of a 23andMe genomics file is
  recoverable from lengths, and a single-SNP chunk's compressed length can fully determine
  the base (A:7051, C:7050, T:7051, G:7050). The independent convergent-encryption literature
  says the same thing from the other side: "if the exact size of a file is divulged then this
  information can be used for a confirmation-of-a-file attack." **Length is a value.** Presence
  and bounded count are far weaker but are not free either.
- **Measured low-entropy reversal, best numbers found.** Entire worldwide mobile phone number
  space (≈118 billion, ≈2^37 after preprocessing) swept in ≈65 s for SHA-1 and <≈300 s for
  SHA-256 with John the Ripper on one consumer GPU; amortised <1 ms per hash. Full 2^48 MAC
  address space in 2.6 days on one 2014-era GPU, or ~1 day on another, and structure reduces
  it to ≈2^38; discriminating a pseudonym's vendor takes 2–8 seconds. Full 2^32 IPv4 space on
  an ordinary laptop in 2 min 16 s. 10^9 SSNs in ~5.5 hours on an old laptop. 77% of US first
  names from a 2,000-entry list in a fraction of a second. A German federal agency's
  MD5/SHA-1-hashed 3,000-URL blocklist: 99% of cleartext entries recovered. **Every value this
  design would want to commit to — hostname, path, timestamp, argv fragment, enum — is
  smaller than every one of those domains.**
- **The three identities want three different answers, and uniformity here is a mistake.**
  Decision identity: public collision-resistant digest over an injective tuple encoding — it
  must be independently re-derivable by a future run with no secret, which forecloses keying
  it. Document identity: a prefix-determined random token minted at header time, not a digest
  of anything. Opaque-field commitment: nothing in plain; in rich, the AEAD tag already
  commits and no separate commitment is warranted. The reason they differ is that they answer
  different questions — "would this run decide the same way", "is this the same receipt", and
  "was this the same value" are three predicates with three different verifiers.
- **`ask-identity-under-partial-damage` resolves decisively against a whole-file digest, and
  it is the deciding criterion its author suspected it was.** A digest over the whole file is
  not an identity of a damaged document; it is an identity of *some* byte string, and for a
  truncated file it silently identifies a different document. A prefix-determined random token
  in the header gives a damaged document an honest identity: the reader can say "this is
  document `<tok>`, and it is damaged from unit N", which is exactly the report the total,
  bounded reader is required to produce. The shipped precedent is direct: journald randomly
  and uniquely initialises `file_id` at file creation, puts it in the header and in the
  filename, and its stated rule is that a reader "should try hard to handle this gracefully,
  such as skipping over the corrupted data, but allowing access to as much data around it as
  possible" — which is only expressible if identity survives damage. Git is the control case:
  content-addressed identity means a corrupt loose object has *no* identity and is simply
  reported missing.
- **`ask-no-end-of-file-commitment` resolves: identity MUST be prefix-determined, and this
  falls straight out of the previous point plus front 2's binding rule.** A post-hoc
  whole-file digest is admissible only as an *attribute reported on read* ("the bytes I saw
  hash to X"), never as the document's name, never as something bound into any unit, and never
  as the thing a reader matches to decide which document it is holding. The two must not share
  a field or a vocabulary.
- **`ask-directory-listing-as-oracle`: enumeration leaks count, ordering, name length, and
  every semantic byte of the name, before a single byte is read — and this is stated as a
  known limit by a shipped design that encrypts names properly.** fscrypt: "does not protect
  the confidentiality of non-filename metadata, e.g. file sizes, file permissions, file
  timestamps"; the userspace tool adds "the sizes and number of files". fscrypt pads names to a
  32-byte boundary specifically "to reduce leakage of filename lengths via their ciphertexts",
  recommending the largest padding "since this provides the best confidentiality". Names are
  strictly *more* exposed than contents in at least three concrete ways for us: Windows grants
  list-folder as an ACE distinct from read-data; a file mailed to a vendor carries its name as
  the attachment name; and indexers, sync clients and backup catalogues record names far more
  eagerly than contents.
- **On the readable-filename lean: the readable name is not a commitment problem at all, it is
  a disclosure problem, and that reframing is the useful part.** Guess-confirmation only
  applies to a value you meant to withhold. A name containing a hostname has *disclosed* the
  hostname; there is no cryptography left to discuss and no construction that makes it both
  readable-by-owner and opaque-to-a-lister. **Say that plainly: not achievable.** Anything an
  observer can read, an observer has. So the only coherent rule is a scope rule, and there is
  a good one available for free.
- **The scope rule I recommend, and it is checkable rather than a matter of taste: a minted
  name may contain exactly what the rich projection's plaintext skeleton already contains, and
  nothing from the opaque-value-capable set.** The skeleton is already the design's own
  declaration of what it does not consider confidential; reusing that classification for names
  adds no second judgement call and no second thing to get wrong. Concretely that admits
  timestamp, enum/subcommand, generation, and bounded ordinals; it excludes argv fragments,
  script paths, and any host-supplied record bytes. Hostname is the genuinely contested cell —
  see below.
- **Hostname is the contested cell, and the honest answer is that it is a real leak that we
  are choosing to take.** RFC 8117 is unambiguous that hostnames "will always be at least
  partial identifiers", that even a generic name "picked from vocabularies that include
  thousands of potential choices" materially "reduces the scope of the search", and that
  correlation with any auxiliary source closes the gap. For an ops tool the aggregate is worse
  than any single name: a store listing is an inventory of managed infrastructure plus a
  schedule. But RFC 8117 also states our own counter-case in its security considerations —
  "incident response using hostnames to track the source of traffic might be affected. It is
  common practice to include hostnames and reverse lookup information at various times during
  an investigation." That is the firefighter, in the IETF's own words, conceded as the cost of
  the mitigation. So: allow it, and *say* in the documentation that the store directory listing
  is an infrastructure inventory and a schedule, in those words, next to the existing sentence
  that says what encryption here does and does not protect.
- **The name-minting normalisation discipline is right, and it inherits the glue mandate
  wholesale.** Restricting minted names to a strict cross-platform-safe subset is the
  most-restrictive-output analogue of the strict recognizer, and it is sound. But the mapping
  from a real hostname into that subset **must be injective**, or two distinct hosts mint the
  same name, and under front 5's create-exclusively-under-the-final-name rule that is a hard
  publication failure rather than a silent overwrite. Naive lowercasing is not injective
  (`Prod-DB` and `prod-db` collide); neither is character-class stripping. Use an escaping
  encoding, or accept non-injectivity and carry a disambiguator — and note that the
  disambiguator must then be the prefix-determined random token, because it cannot be a digest
  of content that has not been written yet.
- **`ask-name-alphabet-constraint` survives the encoding constraint but truncation changes the
  argument in a way that must be stated, not waved at.** For a *decision digest* used as a
  name, truncation to L bits gives collision resistance min(2^{L/2}, 2^128) and preimage
  min(2^L, 2^128) — a pure integrity trade, priced and fine. For anything over a low-entropy
  value, truncation does not create hiding: it creates an anonymity set, and the anonymity-set
  model fails whenever the candidates are not equiprobable. The measured collapse is on record
  — a nominal 2^18 anonymity set for truncated MAC pseudonyms fell to 2^17 under one crude
  wired-versus-wireless classification, and Safe Browsing's nominal ~14,757 average anonymity
  set is defeated by multi-prefix and decomposition structure. Signal's own truncation of
  SHA-1 to 10 bytes for contact discovery bought exactly nothing, because 80 bits still
  uniquely determines a member of a 2^37 domain. Rule: truncate integrity digests freely,
  never truncate as a privacy measure.
- **`ask-identity-versus-filename-separation`: keep a stable identity in BOTH places, and bind
  them.** The audit finding that decides this is not about privacy: gocryptfs bound file
  integrity to an internal file ID "and *not* to the file name and/or file path", so
  "exchanging the (encrypted) names of two ciphertext files exchanges their plaintext
  contents". Read across to us: if a document's internal identity is not bound to the name it
  was published under, two whylogs can be renamed past each other and the reader will not
  notice. Since the token is prefix-determined and in the header, putting it in the name too
  costs nothing and makes the mismatch detectable. Selecting "most recent" by name is a
  separate and weaker concern — no platform guarantees enumeration order anyway (front 5), so
  the ordering must come from inside the file or from a monotone component of the name.
- **The glue mandate has a named, boring, correct answer for every identity this design
  computes, and taking it removes the seam entirely.** TupleHash is a standardised
  injective-tuple hash whose whole purpose is that `("abc","d")` and `("ab","cd")` produce
  different outputs; KMAC is the same construction with a key; both take a customization string
  that provides domain separation with the guarantee that different customization strings give
  "unrelated" functions. Same primitive family, one parameter apart, so the decision identity
  and any future keyed use share a construction rather than two hand-rolled encodings. The
  spec explicitly blesses restricting an implementation to one fixed tuple arity and one fixed
  customization string, which is exactly the decision-identity use. Precomputation makes the
  fixed-parameter case as fast as bare SHAKE.
- **The glue failure this replaces is not hypothetical.** Flickr's shipped API signature
  concatenated sorted key/value pairs with no delimiters, so `"foo=bar"` and `"foob=ar"` signed
  identically; combined with a Merkle–Damgård length extension over `MD5(SECRET||m)` that was
  a full forgery. Ten other named services documented the same signing process; one vendor's
  attempted fix did not work. Two glue bugs, one identity computation, seventeen years after
  the underlying attack was published.
- **Deterministic keyed commitments still leak by frequency, which is why "keyed" is not the
  end of the analysis.** Both a peer-reviewed survey and a practitioner walkthrough land on the
  same caveat independently: "since an HMAC is deterministic, two records for the same
  identifier can be linked", and "any hashing scheme that always maps the same input to the
  same output is vulnerable to frequency analysis". So even in the rich projection, a keyed
  field commitment would need per-document salting to avoid becoming a cross-document
  correlation channel — at which point it is doing the job the AEAD tag already does, which is
  the practical argument for not having a separate field commitment at all.
- **Terminology warning for anyone reading the pseudonymisation literature next to our
  corpus.** ENISA's three named policies are deterministic / document-randomized /
  fully-randomized, and its *document-randomized* means randomised **within** a document while
  stable **across** documents — the exact inverse of what our corpus calls a per-document
  salted tag (stable within, unlinkable across). ENISA's utility table scores
  document-randomized as losing statistics and protocol semantics but keeping cross-trace
  comparison. Our construction is a fourth cell not in their taxonomy: per-document-scoped
  deterministic. Do not reuse their label.
- **Two things are good on both axes and should be recorded as the round's easy keeps.** A
  prefix-determined random document token is better for the firefighter (a damaged document
  still has a name to speak about, and `dorc why <token>` works on a partial file) *and* better
  for confidentiality (nothing value-derived) *and* simpler code (no deferred computation, one
  draw from the seam). An injective tuple encoding for the decision identity is better for
  correctness (re-derivation actually means something), better for security (no ambiguity
  class), and cheaper to review (a named standard instead of a bespoke concatenation).

>   l.445-451: the same `file_id` is then used as the KEY for siphash24 over payloads, "as it
>   makes the format more robust against attackers that want to trigger hash collisions" — a
>   per-document random value promoted to a key for an in-document keyed function, which is
>   structurally the construction this front recommends for within-document tags.

> [A-hornby-gocryptfs-audit-2017]:p4 §2.1 (relevance: +1:SURE)
> The problem is that the integrity of the file contents is bound just to the file ID and not
> to the file name and/or file path. Exchanging the (encrypted) names of two ciphertext files
> exchanges their plaintext contents.
> └ decides `ask-identity-versus-filename-separation`: an internal identity that is not bound to
>   the published name permits undetected name-swapping between documents. Put the token in both
>   places and check them against each other.

> [A-hornby-gocryptfs-audit-2017]:p5 §2.3 (relevance: +1:SURE)
> Directory IV Poisoning […] Dropbox: Over time, and at some risk of being detected, Dropbox can
> determine whether files in different directories have the same name.
> └ and from the audit's fuller text: the adversary copies the victim's `gocryptfs.diriv` into a
>   directory she controls and creates files with the same encrypted names. The scope salt was
>   in-band, therefore copyable, therefore the scope was not a scope. Hard bound on what a
>   per-document tag can promise: it is unlinkable ACROSS documents only, and offers nothing to
>   anyone holding the document itself.

> [B-gocryptfs-cryptography-design-2026]:§"File Names", §"Long File Name Handling" (relevance: +1:SURE)
> Every directory gets a 128-bit directory Initialisation Vector (IV) that is stored in each
> directory as gocryptfs.diriv. […] Due to the fixed per-directory IV, file name encryption is
> deterministic in each directory for the lifetime of that directory. […] Because the hash is
> only taken from the encrypted file name that is public anyway, there is no security penalty
> for using long names.
> └ the discriminating rule stated by a shipping project: hashing a value that is ALREADY public
>   costs nothing; hashing a value you meant to withhold is a guess-confirmation oracle. Every
>   question on this front is an instance of deciding which of those two a given field is.

> [A-nist-sp800-185-sha3-derived-2016]:p12 §5.1 (relevance: +1:SURE)

> [A-nist-sp800-185-sha3-derived-2016]:p5 §2.3.1-2.3.2 (relevance: +1:SURE)

> parsed from the beginning of the string by inserting the length of the byte string before the

> [A-nist-sp800-185-sha3-derived-2016]:p19 §8.2.2, p20 §8.3, §8.4.2, p17 §7.1-7.2 (relevance: +1:SURE)

> [A-git-hash-function-transition-2026]:§Background, §"Choice of Hash", §"Object names" (relevance: +1:SURE)
> Integrity checking is easy. Bit flips, for example, are easily detected, as the hash of
> corrupted content does not match its name. […] Object names can be signed and third parties
> can trust the hash to address the signed object and all objects it references. […] The hash
> function's properties should match Git's needs (e.g. Git requires collision and 2nd preimage
> resistance and does not require length extension resistance). […] The SHA-256 name of an
> object is the SHA-256 of the concatenation of its type, length, a nul byte, and the object's
> SHA-256 content.
> └ COUNTER-THESIS at its strongest, from a shipped system: a public collision-resistant digest
>   IS the right identity when the requirement is integrity plus independent verifiability by a
>   third party, and the project enumerates precisely which hash properties it needs. Note also
>   what the design costs: "SHA-256 repositories cannot be read by older versions of Git", a
>   bidirectional translation table, a topological sort during fetch — the price of having made
>   identity depend on content. And note the injective encoding is present (type, length, NUL)
>   for the same reason TupleHash exists.

> [B-nix-content-addressing-outputs-2026]:§"Design rationale" (relevance: -0:SUSPECT)
> The content-addressing of an output only depends on that store object itself, not any other
> information external (such has how it was made, when it was made, etc.). As a consequence, a
> store object will be content-addressed the same way regardless of whether it was manually
> inserted into the store, outputted by some derivation, or outputted by a some other
> derivation. […] regardless of what the builder does during the build, it cannot influence

> through the outputs whose content-addresses are fixed.
> └ COUNTER-THESIS, second form. The argument is entirely about controlled impurity and

## Open, and what would settle it

- **Whether a plain projection should emit within-document tags at all, or nothing.** The tag
  is safe, but it is not free of design cost: it is a second thing readers must understand, and
  it invites the misreading "these are the same value" → "I can compare them to the rich
  document". Settling this needs a user-story pass, not more sources: does the firefighter
  actually reach for equality classes when the values are absent, or does an omission marker
  with a count suffice? I did not pursue it because it is a product question and this front is
  a principles front.
- **Whether the readable-name lean survives a specific adversary the human has in mind.** I
  have argued it is a disclosure decision, not a cryptographic one, and that the cost is a
  legible infrastructure inventory plus schedule in a directory listing. Whether that cost is
  acceptable is a ruling, not a finding. What would settle it: naming the principal we are
  worried about. If it is "another local user on a shared controller", the listing is the whole
  attack and readable names are bad. If it is "a stolen laptop without full-disk encryption"
  (front 4's stated honest scope), the adversary who can list can also read, and readable names
  cost nothing extra.
- **Whether the store needs an ordering channel independent of names.** Front 5 established
  enumeration order is unspecified everywhere. If the name carries a timestamp for readability,
  it also happens to give ordering — but a timestamp is a low-entropy value in the name and
  lexicographic ordering of a timestamp requires a fixed-width zero-padded form. Not pursued;
  it is a format detail for front 9 rather than a principles question.
