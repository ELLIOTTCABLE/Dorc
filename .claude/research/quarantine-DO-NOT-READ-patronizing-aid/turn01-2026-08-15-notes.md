# turn 1 — 2026-08-15 15:40:26 CDT

Scope: prior art at the UX/security intersection, aimed at the Dorc user-aid plane — how to render
material a hostile managed host can influence, to expert operators, without either lying by omission
or dressing attacker-influenced bytes as measurement. Twenty-four graded sources (16 A, 8 B).

## Findings

- Field telemetry over 25.4M warning impressions shows technically-skilled populations click through
  MORE, not less; the paper itself floats "feel patronized by warnings" as a candidate cause.
- Progressive disclosure is near-dead on arrival: 1.6% opened Chrome's "Help me understand", zero
  Firefox users opened "Technical Details", and only 16.8% of notified operators followed a detail
  link. Detail behind a click is, empirically, detail withheld.
- Habituation destroys adherence at *realistic* rates: under five warnings a day, adherence fell
  87%→55% across fifteen working days; varying the warning's appearance held it at 76%; participants
  consciously knowing about the variation did not defeat the effect.
- Comprehension and adherence come apart in both directions — a redesign that failed at comprehension
  still nearly doubled adherence, and correctly understanding a low-risk warning made users MORE
  likely to proceed.
- An expert population trusts static analysis MORE than a general one, not less; expertise correlates
  with higher use and higher belief that the tool catches real faults. Expertise is not skepticism.
- Expert false-positive tolerance is low and quantified: only 24% of developers tolerate a 20% FP
  rate; Google's compile-time bar is under 10% *effective* false positives; an analyzer whose
  "Not useful" click ratio exceeds 10% is auto-disabled.
- "Effective false positive" is the correct metric: a true finding the operator does not understand
  and does not act on is a false positive in practice, and the operator — not the tool author — sets
  the rate.
- A truthful but differently-framed diagnostic steers repair: on one task, 49 participants took the
  wrong fix and 1 took the right one, because the message named the subclass rather than the parent.
  Diagnostic steering is proven in the benign case; the adversarial case is a corollary, not a leap.
- Error messages are read, and are *harder* to read than source code (419ms vs 394ms mean fixations),
  because they force modality-switching between prose and code.
- AF447 is the counter-thesis's strongest artifact: a stall warning designed to self-suppress when its
  measurement source went invalid produced inverted feedback — correct nose-down input made the alarm
  sound, wrong nose-up input silenced it. The design rationale for suppression was, verbatim,
  preventing spurious warnings and protecting the warning's credibility.
- The BEA's remedy was MORE raw material, not fewer warnings: permanent operation of the warning, plus
  display of the underlying angle-of-attack parameter that was never shown to pilots at all.
- Adversaries curate selectively rather than wipe: T1070 documents leaving "sufficient data intact to
  maintain the appearance of normal system behavior", and malware that closes the security dialog the
  operator would otherwise see.
- Operator-console deception is a shipping vulnerability class, currently rated 9.6 CRITICAL in Tomcat
  — where the vendor rated the identical issue "low". The industry has no agreed price for it.
- Explanations increase acceptance of a recommendation *regardless of its correctness*; confidence
  display, not explanation, drives the mental model; and truth-default means that absent an explicit
  suspicion trigger, the diagnosis is simply believed.
- Sysadmins are not the CLI purists folklore claims: 70% of 300 primarily use firewall GUIs, though
  the expert subgroup is least GUI-preferring, and gives verifiability — not power — as its reason.
- Ops doctrine already solves the push/pull split: alert on symptoms, never on causes, and attach the
  currently-firing cause inventory as terse skimmable lines to the one page already sent.
- Cranor's framework already models the attacker as an interference source inside the warning channel,
  and already prescribes passive indicators over active warnings where the hazard is low or
  unactionable — experts get the information without the interrupt.
- Unsolicited, accurate, actionable notification to real operators has a hard ceiling: best case
  +11 points over control, at most 18% remediating, effect dead within about two days, repeat
  notification worthless, and 46% already knew.
- Attacker-supplied remediation advice is named prior art, not speculation: a notification channel
  invites an adversary to report a real issue and prescribe a malicious fix.
- Per-user output customization destroyed static-analysis adoption at Google; because each user saw a
  different view, no issue could be relied on to have been seen. Configuration moved to project scope.

> Over the course of the three-week experiment, average adherence substantially dropped from 87

> [A-vance-tuning-out-security-warnings-2018]:§Discussion (relevance: +1:SURE)
> Overall, accuracy in the polymorphic condition dropped from 87 percent at the start of the three
> weeks to 76 percent at the end. In contrast, accuracy in the static condition dropped from 87
> percent to 55 percent.

Exposure rate was low and realistic — "an average of 4.74 (SD = 1.29) permission warnings viewed per

### "Effective false positive": the metric that reframes the whole aid plane

> [A-sadowski-static-analysis-google-2018]:§Terminology (relevance: +1:SURE)
> We consider an issue to be an "effective false positive" if developers did not take positive action
> after seeing the issue. If an analysis incorrectly reports an issue, but developers make the fix
> anyway to improve code readability or maintainability, that is not an effective false positive. If
> an analysis reports an actual fault, but the developer did not understand the fault and therefore
> took no action, that is an effective false positive.

> [A-sadowski-static-analysis-google-2018]:§sidebar (relevance: +1:SURE)
> Developers, not tool authors, will determine and act on a tool's perceived false-positive rate.

Hard governance thresholds actually in production:

> [A-sadowski-static-analysis-google-2018]:§Criteria for compiler checks (relevance: +1:SURE)
> Produce less than 10% effective false positives. Developers should feel the check is pointing out an
> actual issue at least 90% of the time

> [A-sadowski-static-analysis-google-2018]:§Iterate on feedback (relevance: +1:SURE)
> The Tricorder team tracks such not-useful bugs, computing the ratio of "Please fix" vs. "Not useful"
> clicks. If the ratio for an analyzer goes above 10%, the Tricorder team disables the analyzer until
> the author(s) improve it.

> [A-sadowski-static-analysis-google-2018]:§Lessons (relevance: +1:SURE)
> we have worked to regain the trust of Google developers, finding Google developers have a strong
> bias to ignore static analysis, and any false positives or poor reporting give them a justification
> for inaction.

Timing determines perceived truth, which bears on probe-time versus post-hoc surfacing:

> [A-sadowski-static-analysis-google-2018]:§Reporting issues sooner is better (relevance: +1:SURE)
> survey participants deemed 74% of the issues flagged at compile time as "real problems," compared to
> 21% of those found in checked-in code.

And the direct warning against per-user verbosity dials:

> [A-sadowski-static-analysis-google-2018]:§Project-level customization (relevance: +1:SURE)

> customization caused discrepancies within and across teams and resulted in declining use of tools.
> Because each user could see a different view of issues, there was no way to ensure that a particular

### The counter-thesis, at full strength: AF447

Design intent, verbatim:

> [A-bea-af447-final-report-2012]:§1.6.11, p.43 (relevance: +1:SURE)
> If the CAS measurements for the three ADR are lower than 60 kt, the angle of attack values of the
> three ADR are invalid and the stall warning is then inoperative. This results from a logic stating
> that the airflow must be sufficient to ensure a valid measurement by the angle of attack sensors,
> especially to prevent spurious warnings.

That is the "don't render what your measurement source can no longer vouch for" rule, in a
safety-critical system, stated by its designers. Outcome:

> [A-bea-af447-final-report-2012]:§1.1, p.23 (relevance: +1:SURE)
> During the following seconds, all of the recorded speeds became invalid and the stall warning
> stopped, after having sounded continuously for 54 seconds. The altitude was then about 35,000 ft,
> the angle of attack exceeded 40 degrees

> [A-bea-af447-final-report-2012]:§2.3.3, p.190 (relevance: +1:SURE)
> Several nose-down inputs caused a drop in the pitch attitude and the angle of attack, whose values
> then became valid, such that a clear nose-down input resulted in the triggering of the stall warning.
> It appears that the PF reacted, on at least two occasions, with a nose-up input, whose consequences
> were an increase in angle of attack, a drop in measured speed and consequently stopping the stall
> warning.

The suppression logic inverted the feedback: doing the right thing turned the alarm ON. The
investigator's own conclusion runs directly against curation:

> [A-bea-af447-final-report-2012]:§2.3.3, p.190 (relevance: +1:SURE)
> the drop in measured airspeeds to values of less than 60 kt during the stall caused the repeated
> activation and deactivation of the warning which may have made it considerably more difficult for
> the Captain to effectively analyse the situation on his return to the cockpit. […] There can be
> other conditions where airspeed measurements (severe icing, obstruction by particles) may be
> disrupted and for which it might be beneficial to have a stall warning permanently in operation.

> [A-bea-af447-final-report-2012]:§2.3.3, p.189 (relevance: +1:SURE)
> it is likely that the presentation of information that provides an overview of the aeroplane's
> situation (angle of attack, energy balance (kinetic and potential), flight envelope) would help
> pilots to "make sense" of the warning and to take the appropriate corrective action in time.

And the never-shown underlying measurement:

> [A-bea-af447-final-report-2012]:§3.1 Findings, p.198 (relevance: +1:SURE)
> By design, when the measured speed values are lower than 60 kt, the measured angle of attack values
> are invalidated. […] The aeroplane's angle of attack is not directly displayed to the pilots.

The pro-curation argument the same report records, in fairness — this is the strongest statement of
the other side, made by the same investigators:

> [A-bea-af447-final-report-2012]:§2.3.3, p.189 (relevance: +1:SURE)
> such spurious triggering may be considered as inappropriate and likely to impair the overall
> credibility of a warning which is almost never encountered by crews during type rating, in flight or
> during training.

### The camouflage irony, and the followability constraint

> [A-bainbridge-ironies-of-automation-1983]:p.776 (relevance: +1:SURE)
> automatic control can 'camouflage' system failure by controlling against the variable changes, so
> that trends do not become apparent until they are beyond control. This implies that the automatics
> should also monitor unusual variable movement.

> [A-bainbridge-ironies-of-automation-1983]:p.777 (relevance: +1:SURE)
> if the human operator must monitor the details of computer decision making then, ironically, it is
> necessary for the computer to make these decisions using methods and criteria, and at a rate, which
> the operator can follow, even when this may not be the most efficient method technically. If this is
> not done then the operator will not be able to trace through the system's decision sequence to see
> how far he does agree.

Aid that is merely appended is not free — it can be net-negative:

> [A-bainbridge-ironies-of-automation-1983]:p.777 (relevance: -0:SUSPECT)
> Ephrath (1980) has reported a study in which system performance was worse with computer aiding,
> because the operator made the decisions anyway, and checking the computer added to his workload.

Against static, profile-based curation:

> [A-bainbridge-ironies-of-automation-1983]:p.778 (relevance: +1:SURE)
> It could therefore confuse rather than help the operator to give him a display which is solely a
> function of his overall skill level. Non-time-stressed operators, if they find they have the wrong
> type of display, might themselves request a different level of information. This would add to the
> work load of someone making decisions which are paced by a dynamic system.

> [A-bainbridge-ironies-of-automation-1983]:p.778 (relevance: +1:SURE)
> it is possible for an interface which is ideal for normal conditions to camouflage the development
> of abnormal ones

### Honesty, provenance, and the limits of epistemic labelling

The framework already contains the threat model:

> [A-cranor-human-in-the-loop-2008]:§2.2 (relevance: +1:SURE)
> Interference is anything that may prevent a communication from being received as the sender
> intended. Interference may be caused by malicious attackers, technology failures, or environmental
> stimuli that obscure the communication.

> [A-cranor-human-in-the-loop-2008]:§2.2 (relevance: +1:SURE)
> Is the indicator resistant to attacks designed to deceive the software into displaying an
> inappropriate indicator? Can the indicator be spoofed, obscured, or otherwise manipulated so that
> users are deceived into relying on an indicator provided by an attacker rather than one provided by
> their system?

And the passive-indicator prescription, which is precisely the push/pull cut:

> [A-cranor-human-in-the-loop-2008]:§2.2 (relevance: +1:SURE)
> frequent, active warnings about relatively low-risk hazards or hazards that ordinary users are unable
> to take action to avoid may lead users to start ignoring not only these warnings, but also similar
> warnings about more severe hazards. A more passive notice or status indicator might be a better
> choice than a warning in such situations, as it will provide information that may be of use to
> expert users without interrupting ordinary users for whom it is of minimal use. On the other hand,
> when hazards are severe and user action is critical, active warnings may be most appropriate

> [A-cranor-human-in-the-loop-2008]:§2.3.4 (relevance: +1:SURE)
> expert users with computer-security-related training and experience may be more likely to understand
> complicated instructions than novice users. On the other hand, experts may also be more likely to
> second-guess security warnings and, perhaps erroneously, conclude that the situation is less risky
> than it actually is.

The reason a warning exists at all is user-held knowledge the system lacks — an argument against
deciding on the operator's behalf:

> [B-reeder-neat-spruce-2011]:§SPRUCE, "Unique knowledge user has" (relevance: +1:SURE)
> Warnings often occur because the engineer expects the user to have some specific contextual
> information that the system does not.

> [B-reeder-neat-spruce-2011]:§SPRUCE, "Evidence" (relevance: +1:SURE)
> Any information the user should factor into their decision; e.g., if this is a decision about whether
> to run a program, the program's publisher is an important piece of evidence.

Positive indicators measurably do nothing, and were withdrawn on that basis:

> [A-chromium-ev-to-page-info-2019]:§rationale (relevance: +1:SURE)
> the Chrome Security UX team has determined that the EV UI does not protect users as intended […]
> Users do not appear to make secure choices (such as not entering password or credit card information)
> when the UI is altered or removed, as would be necessary for EV UI to provide meaningful protection.
> Further, the EV badge takes up valuable screen real estate, can present actively confusing company
> names in prominent UI, and interferes with Chrome's product direction towards neutral, rather than
> positive, display for secure connections.

Contrast: the supply-chain world is currently going the other way, with a positive badge —

> [B-github-npm-provenance-2023]:§Verification (relevance: -0:SUSPECT)
> Packages published with provenance will be shown with a new badge next to the version number in the
> npmjs.com UI

— while being explicit that identity is not semantic trust:

> [B-github-npm-provenance-2023]:§Linking packages (relevance: +1:SURE)
> trust in the source code does not translate into trust in the published package. In order to increase
> the level of trust you have in the npm packages you download from the registry you must have
> visibility into the process by which the source was translated into the published artifact.

### Explanation itself is an action-driver, not a neutral service

> [B-bansal-ai-explanations-2021]:§Abstract (relevance: +1:SURE)
> While we observed complementary improvements from AI augmentation, they were not increased by
> explanations. Rather, explanations increased the chance that humans will accept the AI's
> recommendation, regardless of its correctness.

> [B-bansal-ai-explanations-2021]:§5 pilot observations (relevance: +1:SURE)
> we also observed that explanations increased reliance on recommendations even when they were
> incorrect: explaining the predicted class slightly improved performance (compared to confidence only)
> when the recommendation was correct but decreased performance when it was incorrect.

The mechanism, which is the design lever:

> [B-bansal-ai-explanations-2021]:§5 (relevance: +1:SURE)
> These user behaviors are similar to observations in psychology literature on Truth-Default Theory
> [47], which shows that people exhibit truth-default behavior: by default, people are biased to assume
> that the speaker is being truthful, especially when triggers that raise suspicion are absent.

Confidence, not explanation, is what the mental model is built from:

> [B-bansal-ai-explanations-2021]:§5 (relevance: +1:SURE)
> the AI's confidence is a simple, yet salient feature via which users create a mental model of the
> AI's global behavior […] across all three domains, the same proportion of participants self-reported
> using AI's confidence scores regardless of whether they saw explanations

Carry the population caveat explicitly:

> [B-bansal-ai-explanations-2021]:§6.1 (relevance: +1:SURE)
> we restricted ourselves to tasks amenable to crowdsourcing (text classification and question
> answering), so our results may not generalize to high-stakes domains with expert users such as
> medical diagnosis. We also note that the effectiveness of explanations may depend on user expertise,
> a factor that we did not explore.

### Alert fatigue in the operator population, and the vocabulary problem

> [A-alahmadi-soc-false-positives-2022]:§6 (relevance: +1:SURE)
> when the analyst reported a 99% FP rate, this is found to be mostly benign triggers and not
> necessarily a measurement of the performance of the technology itself.

> [A-alahmadi-soc-false-positives-2022]:§6 (relevance: +1:SURE)
> False alarms are used to describe an alarm generated without an actual security-related event (the
> boy who cried wolf). In contrast, benign triggers are real alarms, meaning they match an existing
> signature but the organization chooses to ignore them for a "business-justified" purpose.

The consequence of fatigue is that automation gets switched off:

> [A-alahmadi-soc-false-positives-2022]:§1 (relevance: +1:SURE)
> The deployed technology could have automatically responded by deleting the malicious file, a
> capability that had been disabled by Target's security personnel. This configuration is not unusual
> as, due to a lack of trust caused by the prevalence of FP alarms created by detection tools, most
> SOCs want to avoid any automated decision that could result in business disruption.

Unexplained verdicts destroy trust, in the operators' own words:

> [A-alahmadi-soc-false-positives-2022]:§7.2 (relevance: +1:SURE)
> So that's one issue of traditional IPS/IDS, that we also don't really have high respect for, because
> if you don't tell me the reason you produce an event for me, you're not ready to open up the reason
> you produce an event for me, and I have only a very high level of description that may not be usable,
> it's not usually usable, let alone actionable, then I can't have really high respect on that.

And the counterweight, from the same population:

> [A-alahmadi-soc-false-positives-2022]:§7.1 (relevance: +1:SURE)
> Downsides may be sometimes too much information.

### Ops doctrine already has the push/pull answer

> [B-ewaschuk-philosophy-on-alerting-2015]:§Introduction (relevance: +1:SURE)
> Every time my pager goes off, I should be able to react with a sense of urgency. I can only do this a
> few times a day before I get fatigued. Every page should be actionable; simply noting "this paged
> again" is not an action.

> [B-ewaschuk-philosophy-on-alerting-2015]:§Causes are still useful (relevance: +1:SURE)
> Print a terse summary of all of your cause-based rules that are firing in every page that you send
> out. A quick skim by a human can identify whether the symptom they just got paged for has an
> already-identified cause. […] Using this approach, the mental burden of the mistuned, noisy rules has
> been changed from a pager beep & ack (and investigation, and followup, and..) to a single line of text
> to be skimmed over.

> [B-ewaschuk-philosophy-on-alerting-2015]:§Tracking & Accountability (relevance: +1:SURE)
> Alerts that are less than 50% accurate are broken; even those that are false positives 10% of the time
> merit more consideration.

> [B-ewaschuk-philosophy-on-alerting-2015]:§Summary (relevance: +1:SURE)
> Err on the side of removing noisy alerts – over-monitoring is a harder problem to solve than
> under-monitoring.

The process-industry version, with the numbers:

> [B-hse-better-alarm-handling-2000]:step 1 (relevance: +1:SURE)
> In the last 11 minutes before the explosion the two operators had to recognise, acknowledge and act on
> 275 alarms.

> [B-hse-better-alarm-handling-2000]:step 3 (relevance: +1:SURE)
> As a long-term average, operators should not have to deal with more than one alarm every ten minutes.
> Following a major plant upset, no more than ten alarms should be displayed in the first ten minutes.

> [B-hse-better-alarm-handling-2000]:step 2 (relevance: +1:SURE)
> process status indicators should not be designated as alarms

### The ceiling on unsolicited expert action

> [A-li-vulnerability-notifications-2016]:§7 (relevance: +1:SURE)
> An additional 11% of contacts addressed the security issue when notified in this fashion, compared to
> the control. However, we failed to prompt the majority of contacts to respond, and even when they did,
> remediation was often only partial. Repeat notifications did not further improve remediation.

> [A-li-vulnerability-notifications-2016]:§4 (relevance: +1:SURE)
> it is important to note that our best notification regimen resulted in at most 18% of the population
> remediating.

Verbose beat terse, and reduced follow-up questions:

> [A-li-vulnerability-notifications-2016]:§4.2 (relevance: +1:SURE)
> For IPv6, verbose messages were 56.5% more effective than either terse messages after two days and
> 55.5% more effective for ICS. […] We note that verbose messages both reduced follow-up communication
> and resulted in the highest patching rate.

(Caveat, stated by the authors: the verbosity difference was not statistically significant under
Bonferroni correction for IPv6 and ICS.)

The benign-trigger pattern reappears in an operator population — the tool is right, the operator knows
better, and the finding is still noise to them:

> [A-li-vulnerability-notifications-2016]:§5.1 (relevance: +1:SURE)
> Twelve IPv6 contacts rebutted our claim of vulnerability. Six stated that the inconsistency was
> intentional; one was a honeypot; and five explained that the IP addresses we sent them no longer
> pointed to the same dual-stack host, likely due to network churn.

Deviating from the expected message form read as an attack:

> [A-li-vulnerability-notifications-2016]:§4.3 (relevance: +1:SURE)
> Several survey respondents were surprised at receiving translated messages from United States
> institutions and initially suspected our notifications were phishing messages or spam, which may
> explain the lower patch rate.
